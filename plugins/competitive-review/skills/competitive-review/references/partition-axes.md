# Partition axes for a competitive fan-out

Generic research partitions — by era, by source registry — carve a competitive category badly. They
produce slices that overlap at exactly the places where positioning lives. These axes are the ones
that cut cleanly.

Pick **one** primary axis. Mixing two produces overlapping slices and duplicate entries at the seams.

## By market slice — the default

Each agent owns one functional slice of the category: the incumbents in one delivery niche, the
specialists in another, the adjacent tools that solve a neighbouring problem.

- **Best when** the category is fragmented and no single player does everything. This is the common
  case, and it is the axis that produces the `slice → owner → our stance` table directly.
- **Watch for** a slice with no owner. That is either a gap worth taking or a sign the slice is not
  real — decide which, explicitly.

## By delivery model

SaaS · self-hosted open source · self-hosted commercial · desktop · library or CLI.

- **Best when** your own differentiation *is* a delivery choice — self-hostable, single binary, no
  egress. The comparison then lands on the axis your thesis actually rests on.
- **Watch for** vendors spanning two models (open core, free tier plus enterprise). Assign them to
  the model that governs the comparison and note the other.

## By adjacency ring

Direct peers → adjacent tools → complements → cautionary tales (the dead).

- **Best when** the category boundary itself is unclear and you need to establish who is even a
  competitor. Also the best axis for a *first* review in a new space.
- **Watch for** the complements ring being treated as competition. A tool you integrate with is not a
  rival, and framing it as one distorts the whole document.

## By era of entry

Incumbents → the challenger generation → what launched in the last 18 months.

- **Best when** the category has recently re-sorted and the newest entrants are the real threat. Also
  the axis that surfaces the "this went stale six months ago" finding.
- **Watch for** era slices being uneven. The recent slice is usually the thinnest in count and the
  most important in consequence — do not lower its floor just because it returns fewer names.

## Slices that always earn a seat

Whatever the primary axis, two slices are worth carving out explicitly, because a general search
under-returns both:

- **The dead and the archived.** Nobody lists them, and each one carries a lesson about the category.
- **The commercial ceiling.** The most expensive, most polished vendor in the space defines the
  quality bar and the pricing contrast, even when it is not a realistic head-to-head rival.

## Sizing

Aim for **4–6 slices**. Fewer and the fan-out is not earning its overhead; more and the seams
multiply faster than the coverage improves. Every slice needs a numeric floor in its prompt, or
agents stop at the three names everybody knows.
