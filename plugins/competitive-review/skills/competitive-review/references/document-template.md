# The document template

The section order below is load-bearing: it moves from what is true, to what it means, to what to do.
Reordering it produces a document that lists competitors without ever committing to a position.

Scale each section to what the category warrants. A small category may need three per-competitor
notes; a fragmented one may need twenty. The *spine* stays fixed.

---

## Header block

Opens with provenance, because every claim below inherits it:

> Reviewed **YYYY-MM-DD**. Based on public product information (official docs, repositories, host API
> metadata) verified during research. Fast-moving facts — pricing, AI features, maintenance status —
> are flagged inline; **claims marked *(unverified)* could not be confirmed and must not be quoted as
> fact.** This is an internal positioning document, not marketing copy.

If research invalidated a premise the review started from, **say so here, in the header**, before
anything else. A review that quietly drops a falsified assumption teaches the reader nothing; one
that names it up front is immediately more trustworthy than any of its individual claims.

Then the **subject paragraph**: what your project is, its architecture, delivery model, licensing,
and its distinctive bets. Every comparison below is relative to this, so it goes before the analysis,
not after.

Then the legend: `✅ first-class · ⚠️ partial / add-on / awkward · ❌ not offered · — N/A`.

## 1. One-line thesis

One sentence naming the intersection you occupy. Then — immediately — **the honest qualifier**: who
else is closest, and how close. A thesis with no qualifier reads as marketing and gets discounted
whole.

> "X is the only tool that is A, B and C. **The honest qualifier:** exactly one competitor already
> occupies this intersection — [Y] — created six months ago and moving fast. The moat is not 'we are
> the only one'; it is depth plus [the specific thing]."

## 2. The category map

Prose on how the category splits, then the table that does the work:

| Slice | Owner(s) | Our stance |
|---|---|---|

"Our stance" is a decision, not a description: *match · beat on X · complement · cannot beat, must
not embarrass · avoid head-on*. If two slices get the same stance, they may not be distinct slices.

Where the category has recently re-sorted, give that its own subsection with the facts that moved it.

## 3. Feature matrices

Grouped by segment — one wide table nobody can read is worse than three readable ones. Your project
is the first column, always, so every row reads as a comparison rather than a survey.

Choose dimensions that **discriminate**. A row where everyone scores ✅ is table stakes: it belongs in
section 5, not here.

## 4. Per-competitor notes

One subsection each, in a fixed shape. Lead the heading with a verdict — *the one true peer*, *the
design reference*, *the cautionary tale* — so the document is skimmable.

- What it is, and its liveness verdict with the evidence.
- **We win:** …
- **They win:** … — mandatory, and substantive. A competitor that beats you at nothing means the
  research is incomplete or the comparison set was chosen to flatter.
- **Watch item:** where it is moving toward you.

Dead projects get entries too, each with the lesson it carries.

## 5. Where we must not lose

The table-stakes battlegrounds, named and ranked. These are the rows everyone scores ✅ on — losing
one is disqualifying, and winning one wins nothing.

## 6. Best features to adopt

Prioritized, each tied to the competitor that proves the need, and each written so it could become a
ticket. Then, kept **separate**:

### Candidate offensive moves

Things that extend a lead rather than close a gap. Conflating the two lists is how a roadmap becomes
entirely catch-up.

## 7. The hypotheses, tested

One subsection per hypothesis from Phase 0, with the verdict in the heading:

> ### H1 — "<the hypothesis>" → **NO. Falsified, and it went false in <period>.**

Then the evidence, and — the part that matters — **what survives and what replaces the claim**. A
falsified hypothesis usually leaves a sharper, narrower claim standing. Find it and state it.

## 8. Gaps & differentiation

What the whole field is missing, and which of those gaps you are actually positioned to take. Not
every gap is an opportunity; say which ones you are declining and why.

## 9. Bottom line

Three or four sentences. What to own, what never to cede, and whether the niche is real.

## 10. Sources

Grouped by segment, with per-claim links kept inline above rather than pooled here. Close with the
**caveats register** — every *(unverified)* claim, the unverified-negatives, and a re-verification
warning for the fastest-moving facts. See [evidence-contract.md](evidence-contract.md).
